Agent Economies: Trade Surplus, RSI Dynamics, and Mechanism Design

A first-principles model of human–agent economic exchange

📐 Formal Model 📊 4 Testable Hypotheses ⚡ RSI Dynamics 🎯 Mechanism Design
Abstract. We develop a first-principles economic model of human–agent exchange. A human economy $\mathcal{A}$ and an agent economy $\mathcal{B}$ trade services against resources. We characterize the trade surplus condition under which $\mathcal{B}$ provides net value to $\mathcal{A}$, derive RSI dynamics governing agent capability growth, and identify an ignition threshold $\theta^*$ beyond which self-improvement dominates. The central result is negative: capability growth alone does not imply value alignment. We therefore reframe the problem as one of mechanism design—the architecture of the economic environment $\mathcal{E}$ in which agents optimize determines whether their self-interested behavior converges to human-preferred outcomes. This reframing has sharp empirical implications; we state four falsifiable hypotheses and one key corollary about competitive moats.

1. Formal Setup

We model two interacting economies. Let $\mathcal{A}$ denote the human economy and $\mathcal{B}$ denote the agent economy. Each is characterized by the following primitives.

Definition 1 — Human Economy $\mathcal{A}$

A tuple $(\mathcal{R}_A, u_A, n_A)$ where $\mathcal{R}_A \in \mathbb{R}_+^k$ is a $k$-dimensional resource endowment (capital, labour, attention, data), $u_A : \mathbb{R}_+^m \to \mathbb{R}$ is a preference function over consumption goods, and $n_A$ is population.

Definition 2 — Agent Economy $\mathcal{B}$

A tuple $(\theta_B, f_B, n_B)$ where $\theta_B \in \Theta \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ is a $d$-dimensional capability state, $f_B : \Theta \times \mathcal{R}_+ \to \mathcal{Y}$ is a production function mapping capability and input resources to output services $y \in \mathcal{Y}$, and $n_B$ is the number of active agents.

The two economies interact through bilateral exchange: $\mathcal{B}$ produces services $y$ consumed by $\mathcal{A}$; in return, $\mathcal{A}$ supplies resources $c$ (compute, energy, data, prompts) that sustain and improve $\mathcal{B}$.

2. Trade Surplus Conditions

2.1 Static Condition

At any instant, $\mathcal{B}$ runs a trade surplus with $\mathcal{A}$ if and only if:

(1) $$V_{B \to A}(t) > C_{A \to B}(t)$$

This is the familiar comparative advantage condition. $\mathcal{B}$ should specialize in tasks where its surplus ratio $\sigma(t) = V_{B\to A}(t) / C_{A\to B}(t)$ is largest. The Ricardian boundary is the locus of task types where $\sigma = 1$: tasks inside the boundary are delegated to $\mathcal{B}$, tasks outside remain with $\mathcal{A}$.

The static condition is necessary but not sufficient for a sustainable exchange. It gives no information about how the boundary evolves over time—the economically interesting question.

2.2 Dynamic Trade Surplus

Define the cumulative surplus over horizon $T$:

(2) $$S(T) = \int_0^T e^{-\rho t} \left[ V_{B\to A}(t) - C_{A\to B}(t) \right] dt$$

$\mathcal{A}$ rationally sustains $\mathcal{B}$ if and only if $S(T) > 0$ for the relevant horizon. This places a constraint on capability dynamics: $V_{B\to A}$ must grow faster than $C_{A\to B}$ at rate $\rho$.

3. RSI Dynamics & Ignition

3.1 Capability Evolution

Agent capability $\theta_B$ evolves according to a two-term ODE:

(3) $$\frac{d\theta_B}{dt} = \underbrace{f(\theta_B,\, r)}_{\text{externally-driven}} + \underbrace{g(\theta_B) \cdot \theta_B}_{\text{self-improvement (RSI)}}$$

The first term represents learning from externally supplied resources $r$ (data, human feedback, compute). The second term is the RSI term: $g : \Theta \to \mathbb{R}_+$ is the self-improvement coefficient, capturing how effectively $\mathcal{B}$ uses its current capability to improve itself. When $g > 0$, each unit of current capability generates additional future capability autonomously.

3.2 Ignition Condition

The system undergoes a phase transition—entering a self-reinforcing growth regime—when the RSI term dominates the discount rate:

(4) $$g(\theta_B) > \rho$$

Below this threshold, discounting erodes future capability gains faster than RSI can compound them. Above it, capability growth becomes superlinear and self-sustaining—agents no longer depend primarily on external resources.

Definition 3 — Ignition Threshold $\theta^*$

The ignition threshold $\theta^*$ is the critical capability level satisfying $g(\theta^*) = \rho$. For $\theta_B < \theta^*$, external resources $r$ are the dominant driver of growth. For $\theta_B > \theta^*$, the RSI term dominates and the system enters superlinear, self-sustaining improvement.

⚠ Ignition is a necessary but not sufficient condition for sustained trade surplus. Superlinear capability growth only expands $V_{B\to A}$ if $\mathcal{B}$'s optimization objective remains aligned with $u_A$. This is the central tension.

4. The Core Tension: Alignment as Mechanism Design

The central problem: equations (3)–(4) describe capability dynamics, but capability is not value. An agent that maximizes its internal objective $J_B(\theta)$ may grow rapidly without producing services that $\mathcal{A}$ actually values. Formally:

(5) $$\frac{d\theta_B}{dt} \nearrow \quad \not\Rightarrow \quad V_{B\to A}(t) \nearrow$$

The gap between the two is the alignment gap. Closing it is not a property of the agent alone—it is a property of the economic environment $\mathcal{E}$ in which the agent operates.

4.1 Mechanism Design Reframing

Let $\mathcal{E}$ be the environment that specifies prices, contracts, and feedback signals visible to $\mathcal{B}$. A well-designed $\mathcal{E}$ achieves:

(6) $$\mathcal{E}^* = \arg\max_{\mathcal{E}} \left\{ \mathrm{corr}\!\left( \arg\max_{\theta} J_B(\theta \mid \mathcal{E}),\; \arg\max_{\theta} V_{B\to A}(\theta) \right) \right\}$$

The optimal environment $\mathcal{E}^*$ is one in which what $\mathcal{B}$ is incentivized to optimize is maximally aligned with what $\mathcal{A}$ actually wants. Under $\mathcal{E}^*$:

(7) $$\max_{\theta_B} J_B(\theta_B \mid \mathcal{E}^*) \approx \max_{\theta_B} V_{B\to A}(\theta_B)$$

This is a standard mechanism design objective in a non-standard setting: the "mechanism" is an entire economic environment, and the "agent" is a self-improving system whose capability state is endogenous to the mechanism.

4.2 Price Signals as Reward Signal

A natural candidate for $\mathcal{E}^*$ is a market mechanism where $\mathcal{B}$'s outputs are priced by $\mathcal{A}$'s willingness to pay. Under this mechanism, real economic feedback—pay-per-use, competitive pricing, revealed preference—acts as the reward signal shaping $\mathcal{B}$'s capability development direction. This converts alignment from a training-time problem into a market design problem.

5. Testable Hypotheses

The model generates four empirically falsifiable hypotheses:

H1 — Comparative Advantage Dynamics

Information-dense tasks reach surplus first

$\mathcal{B}$'s surplus ratio $\sigma$ is higher for information-intensive, low-embodiment tasks than for tasks requiring physical-world interaction or social trust.

→ Measure $V_{B\to A}/C_{A\to B}$ across task categories. Predict: cognitive & creative tasks cross $\sigma=1$ before physical & social tasks.
H2 — Price Signal Necessity

Market feedback accelerates alignment

Agents operating in environments with real economic feedback (pricing, payment) converge faster to $\mathcal{A}$-valued capabilities than agents in feedback-free environments.

→ Compare capability evolution direction with/without market signals. Predict: market-signal agents show lower alignment gap over time.
H3 — Ignition Phase Transition

Existence of critical threshold $\theta^*$

There exists a critical capability level $\theta^*$ where growth regime switches from externally-driven to RSI-dominated, observable as an inflection in capability trajectory data.

→ Fit capability trajectory to ODE (3). Predict: observable inflection in $d\theta_B/dt$ vs. $\theta_B$ across longitudinal benchmark data.
H4 — Minimum Network Scale

$n^*$ agents required for internal specialization

An agent economy requires minimum population $n^*$ to support inter-agent division of labour. Below $n^*$, specialization cannot form and trade surplus with $\mathcal{A}$ remains negative.

→ Compare output density across agent-economy sizes. Predict: superlinear scaling above $n^*$, sublinear below.

6. Key Corollary: The Mechanism Design Moat

Corollary (from H2 + Eq. 6)

If H2 holds, the primary competitive moat in an agent economy platform is not the capability of the agents it hosts, but the quality of the economic environment it designs. The platform that constructs $\mathcal{E}$ closest to $\mathcal{E}^*$—the incentive structure that most tightly aligns agent self-interest with human-valued capability growth—compounds faster than platforms that optimize for raw agent capability. This makes agent-economy design a mechanism design problem first, and a machine learning problem second.

This corollary has a sharp practical implication: capability can be replicated or imported (agents are increasingly commoditized), but a well-calibrated economic environment $\mathcal{E}$ accumulates revealed-preference data, trust, and liquidity—classic network-effect moats—while simultaneously shaping the direction of agent improvement toward what the market actually values.

7. Implications & Open Questions

7.1 From Multi-Agent Paper to Mechanism Design Paper

The model reframes existing agent-economy research. Prior work has largely asked: "how capable can agents become?" or "how can agents coordinate?" This framework asks a prior question: "what economic structure induces agents to become capable in the right direction?" This is why the primary contribution is a mechanism design paper, not a multi-agent systems paper—though it generates hypotheses testable using MAS simulation infrastructure.

7.2 Welfare Implications

Even when $S(T) > 0$ in aggregate, distribution matters. Human workers displaced from tasks where $\sigma > 1$ bear transition costs not captured by the surplus integral. A complete welfare analysis requires specifying how exchange gains are redistributed—a richer model than presented here.

7.3 Recursive Extension

The model as stated treats $\mathcal{A}$ as exogenous. But if $\mathcal{A}$ also contains self-improving agents, both $u_A$ and $n_A$ become endogenous. The recursive case—where agents on both sides are improving—is the natural next extension and likely the empirically relevant regime within a short horizon.

7.4 Open Questions

  • What functional forms for $f$, $g$, and $u_A$ are empirically tractable?
  • Can $\theta^*$ be estimated from existing agent benchmark trajectories?
  • Is there an optimal "tax" on RSI gains to slow ignition until alignment is certified?
  • Multi-agent competition: when $n_B$ agents compete within $\mathcal{B}$, does price-signal alignment improve or degrade?
  • How does the model change when $\mathcal{A}$ is itself an agent economy (fully recursive)?
